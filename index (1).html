About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Political Science at George Mason University’s Schar School of Policy and Government and an Associate at The Asia Group, where I work on Japanese domestic politics, energy policy, and U.S.–Japan relations.

My research asks how public opinion, party competition, and the factions of the Liberal Democratic Party shape what Japan does on immigration and in the world. I work with mixed methods: survey experiments, quantitative analysis of legislative and party behavior, and interview fieldwork conducted in Japanese in Tokyo, Gunma, and Fukuoka.

My work appears in International Migration Review, the Journal of Conflict Resolution, PS: Political Science & Politics, and Foreign Affairs. With Ming Wan, I am co-author of a Cambridge Element on LDP factional politics and Japan’s economic statecraft.

Featured

Book with Ming Wan. Cambridge University Press, 2025.

Factional Politics in the Liberal Democratic Party: Explaining Change and Continuity in Japan’s Economic Statecraft

Why does Japan’s economic statecraft sometimes lurch in a new direction and then hold steady for years? Drawing on LDP faction membership data from 1961 to 2024, Ming Wan and I trace two cases: Japan’s sweeping sanctions on Russia after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, and its decades-long strategy toward ASEAN. We argue that the balance of power among the party’s factions explains both the sudden shifts and the inertia that follows.

Dissertation

Contested Spaces: Immigration and Domestic Politics in Japan

Chair: Justin Gest. Committee: Lucas Núñez and Naoru Koizumi.

Japan’s population is shrinking and its foreign-born population is growing faster than ever. This three-paper dissertation examines how citizens and politicians respond: what Japanese voters believe about decline and immigration, why those beliefs differ so much from place to place, and how the party that governs Japan coordinates on policy after dismantling its own factions.

  1. Demographic Shock? What Information about Decline Does and Doesn’t Do to Immigration and Fertility Attitudes in Japan

    A survey experiment testing whether information about population decline moves Japanese attitudes on immigration and fertility policy. It does, but unevenly: urban respondents and rural or older respondents respond in opposite directions.

  2. Geography, Demographics, and the Limits of Tolerance: Qualitative Evidence on Immigration and Fertility Attitudes in Japan

    Semi-structured interviews with about sixty residents of Tokyo, Ōizumi, and Fukuoka explain the split in the experiment. A community’s capacity to absorb newcomers, more than contact with them, shapes how people read immigration and demographic decline.

  3. Institutional Stickiness: Voting Behavior of Former Faction Members in the Liberal Democratic Party

    The LDP formally dissolved most of its factions in 2024. Using dyadic analysis of the 2021, 2024, and 2025 party presidential elections, with the Asō faction as a comparison case that never disbanded, this paper tests whether former faction ties still coordinate how members vote.
